The historiography of the Atlantic slave trade has undergone, since the publication of Philip Curtin's census in 1969, a profound methodological reorientation, wherein quantitative reconstruction of transoceanic flows has gradually yielded pride of place to inquiries into the cultural, linguistic, and ritual continuities that enslaved Africans sustained amid dislocation. Scholars such as Sidney Mintz and Richard Price, contesting the proposition that the Middle Passage effected a wholesale erasure of antecedent social forms, advanced the notion of an incipient creole synthesis; others, notably John Thornton, have countered that African political and military institutions remained decisive in shaping both the supply of captives and their subsequent adaptive strategies. That these interpretations remain irreconcilable in part reflects the fragmentary character of the archival record, which privileges the ledgers of merchants over the testimonies of the enslaved themselves.
