The doctrine of promissory estoppel, which emerged from equitable principles to mitigate the rigidity of the consideration requirement, precludes a promisor from resiling from an assurance upon which the promisee has reasonably and detrimentally relied, notwithstanding the absence of any bargained-for exchange. Its application, however, is circumscribed: courts have consistently held that the doctrine operates as a shield rather than a sword, furnishing a defence but not, in the ordinary course, an independent cause of action. Moreover, the promisee's reliance must be both foreseeable to the promisor and sufficiently substantial that injustice could be avoided only by enforcement of the promise. Where the representation is equivocal, or where the promisee has acted in knowledge of its provisional character, the requisite unconscionability is wanting, and relief will be denied.
