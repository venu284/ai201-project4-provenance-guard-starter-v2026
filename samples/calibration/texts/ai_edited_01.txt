Remote work has changed how people think about productivity and place. For decades the office was the default setting for serious work, and employees who wanted flexibility often had to justify asking. The pandemic flipped that assumption almost overnight. Companies found that lots of tasks could be done just as well from a kitchen table as from a cubicle, and workers found out how much time they'd been losing to commutes.

But the shift hasn't been all good. Remote work can blur the line between work and home life, so it's harder to switch off at the end of the day. It can also weaken the casual chats that spark ideas and help new hires learn the culture of a team.

In the end, the best way forward is probably a hybrid approach. Mix focused time at home with planned in-person collaboration, and organizations can get the benefits of both. The key is to be deliberate. Clear expectations, good tools and a real commitment to inclusion will matter far more then the exact number of days in the office.
